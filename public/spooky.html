Two stories for the Syntax Halloween episode

From Failure to Fun

Two not-so-spooky developer stories.

Hey Syntax FM!

I have two stories for your Halloween episode. They’re not exactly spooky stories, but rather stories about turning failure into fun. Both involve monsters in computers, unexpected musical success, and about twenty years between them.

Part one · about twenty years ago

The virus that accidentally created a successful song

About twenty years ago, when Macromedia Flash was still popular, I got a contract to develop a Flash application for a movie website.

I didn’t know much about Flash, but after three weeks of learning and coding, I had a working application.

Then disaster struck.

I accidentally executed some malware. My screen turned grey, my computer started working furiously, and instead of immediately shutting it down, I waited.

Bad idea.

The malware was overwriting my hard drive. My compiled Flash application survived, but the source code was gone. And naturally, I had no backup.

Three weeks of work, lost.

That evening, while desperately checking what files had survived, I discovered a recording a friend had given me featuring a famous German Schlager singer. It contained the line:

„Alles verloren, was ich hab“

“I’ve lost everything I had.”

Perfect.

Since I was also a musician, I sampled that line and wrote a song about losing my work and wishing I could become a sheep farmer instead of a developer. I called it Alles verloren.

Some time later, I performed it in a small underground venue. A drummer friend liked it and asked to develop it further. I agreed, especially since clearing the Schlager sample wasn’t something I wanted to deal with.

And then things got weird.

He entered the song in Austria’s Protest Song Contest, an alternative to Eurovision, and came third! The song got radio airplay, was reworked several times, eventually involved a well-known German DJ and singer Rainer von Vielen, and appeared on compilations and radio best-of collections.

By then, almost nothing of my original song remained except the chorus, which wasn’t even sampled anymore.

And here’s the punchline:

That song earned me more royalties than anything else in my entire musical career.

Not a fortune, but still. A computer virus had accidentally led to my most financially successful song.

Unfortunately, I still had to rewrite that Flash application.

Part two · twenty years later

The monster that escaped the CI pipeline

I’m now a software engineer working on Kibana at Elastic, dealing with something every developer fears: flaky tests.

Our CI pipelines were sometimes so unreliable that we developed a theory: the pipeline was cursed, and a creature called the Flaky Test Monster was responsible.

Then, one weekend while I was on vacation, the flakiness decreased. My colleagues immediately suspected that perhaps I was the Flaky Test Monster.

Naturally, I used AI to confirm this theory. And since AI is always right, the resulting monster looked suspiciously like me.

What started as a joke became an emoji, then a LEGO-style figure, and eventually a 3D-printed monster.

At a local makerspace, I began turning colleagues and entire teams into 3D characters. Eventually, I exhibited the monster and its creations at a maker event, including a webcam-to-3D pipeline that could transform visitors into monsters.

So a fictional creature from our CI pipeline had literally escaped into the physical world.

But it didn’t stop there.

The Flaky Test Monster started making music.

I created songs about flaky tests and the monster’s questionable contributions to software engineering.

Full circle

Twenty years, two failures, two songs

And suddenly, I realized I’d come full circle. Twenty years ago, a computer virus destroyed my code and unexpectedly led to a successful song. Now, flaky tests had created a monster that was dragging me back into making music.

Perhaps the next logical step is entering the Eurovision Song Contest with the Flaky Test Monster. Or maybe producing the world’s first Flaky Test Monster Rock Opera.

Help the monster find a country

After all

Both stories demonstrate something important:

The first software failure became a song. The second became a monster that makes songs.

And maybe that’s the real horror: after twenty years, I’m still a software developer.
